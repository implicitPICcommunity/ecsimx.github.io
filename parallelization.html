Distributed memory

Massively parallel by design

ECsim-X inherits the MPI architecture of ECsim: Cartesian domain decomposition, neighbour-only particle exchange, and a matrix-free GMRES field solver.

Domain decomposition

ECsim splits the simulation box across MPI processes in a Cartesian topology. Each process owns the field values of its subdomain and all particles located inside it. Two classes hold the state: Fields (E on nodes, B on nodes and cell centres, implicit current, mass matrices) and Particles (one instance per species).

Communication in each phase

PhaseWhat is communicatedNotes
Moment gatheringĴ and mass-matrix contributions on boundary nodes are summed across neighbouring processesThe only part of this phase that requires communication across processes
Field solveGhost-node values exchanged with neighbours in every solver sub-iterationPETSc matrix-free GMRES; the main communication cost at scale
Particle moverParticles that leave a subdomain are copied to face buffers and sent with MPI_SendrecvSix buffers per species, initially sized at 5% of the particles per process, resizable

Particle storage

Each species stores seven one-dimensional arrays (three position components, three velocity components, and the charge). The alpha tensors are recomputed in the mover rather than stored, so particle storage dominates memory use.

Where the time goes

In a profile with 512 MPI processes on a 160³ grid (homogeneous 3D electron–ion plasma, 512 particles per species per cell), moment gathering accounted for almost 90% of runtime, and about 75% of that was mass-matrix computation. The field solver and particle mover were almost negligible by comparison. With at least about 100 particles per cell typically required to control noise, this profile is representative of production-style runs.

92%weak-scaling efficiency at 4096 coresSuperMUC, Xeon E5-2680
  • Weak scaling: 20×20×20 cells per process (about 0.5 GB per process), referenced to one node of 16 MPI processes. Efficiency loss comes mainly from communication; moment gathering scales slightly better than the whole code and the field solver scales worst.
  • Strong scaling: near-ideal on MareNostrum with a slight shortfall at 4096 cores. The paper reports two problem sizes (16–512 and 256–4096 cores); its text and figure caption disagree on the grid dimensions, so they are not repeated here.
  • Load balance: well balanced across threads; spin time grows from one node to 32 nodes, which the authors attribute to field-solver communication, their main optimization target.

Source: Gonzalez-Herrero et al. Hardware is from 2017-era systems; performance tests ran 10 cycles.

MPI with GPUs

In the OpenACC port, the MPI structure is unchanged. In the published GPU runs on Leonardo Booster nodes (four NVIDIA A100 per node), each MPI task is bound to one GPU. Moment gathering and the particle mover run on the GPU, while the PETSc field solver and its MPI communication remain on the CPU. See GPU Acceleration for details and Benchmarks for the measured scaling up to 1024 GPUs.

Known limit. The OpenACC paper reports that strong-scaling efficiency falls beyond 64 GPUs on its smallest grid and attributes this to communication overhead, which GPU acceleration can amplify because the computation gets faster.
